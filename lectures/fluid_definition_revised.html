Learning Objectives

  • Explain the mechanical distinction between a fluid and a solid
  • Use the continuum assumption to define fluid fields
  • Define density, specific weight, viscosity, pressure, and compressibility
  • Distinguish absolute, gauge, and vapor pressure
  • Prepare the property model used in fluid statics
Read the outcomes briefly. Emphasize that students will use the concepts in calculations, not only recognize definitions.

Why Fluid Mechanics for Structural Engineers?

  • Hydrostatic loads on tanks, gates, and submerged structures
  • Wind loads and wakes around buildings and bridges
  • Wave, current, and buoyancy effects offshore
  • Pipe, drainage, and hydraulic infrastructure
Ask students to name one fluid load that becomes a structural load. Use their answers to frame the course.

What Is a Fluid?

Transition from applications to the constitutive definition.

Mechanical Definition

A fluid continuously deforms under any applied shear stress.

Solid

  • Can sustain static shear
  • Reaches a finite deformation

Fluid

  • Cannot sustain static shear
  • Deformation continues while shear acts
Stress the word continuously. Do not define a fluid simply as a material that takes the shape of its container.

Think–Pair–Share

Is toothpaste a fluid?

  • Yes
  • No
  • It depends on the applied stress
Give students 60 seconds. Return to the answer after introducing Newtonian and non-Newtonian behaviour. Toothpaste has a yield-stress response and can flow after a threshold is exceeded.

Continuum Assumption

Explain why macroscopic fields are possible although fluids are molecular.

From Molecules to Fields

Average microscopic behaviour over a small material volume.

The volume must be:

  • Large compared with molecular spacing
  • Small compared with the engineering length scale
Define a fluid particle as a material volume element, not a single molecule. Link this to fields p(x,t), rho(x,t), and V(x,t).

Continuum Validity

$$Kn=\frac{\lambda}{L}$$
  • \(\lambda\): molecular mean free path
  • \(L\): characteristic engineering length
  • For \(Kn\ll 1\), continuum modelling is appropriate
Mention Knudsen number only as a validity check. For ordinary water and air problems at civil-engineering scales, the continuum assumption is normally excellent.

Essential Fluid Properties

Focus on properties required immediately in statics and later in dynamics.

Mass-Related Properties

Density

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}$$

Units: kg/m³

Derived quantities

  • Specific weight: \(\gamma=\rho g\)
  • Specific volume: \(v=1/\rho\)
  • Specific gravity: \(SG=\rho/\rho_{ref}\)
Explicitly distinguish specific weight from specific gravity. For water near room temperature, use about 1000 kg/m3 when an engineering approximation is sufficient.

Viscosity: Resistance to Deformation Rate

For a Newtonian fluid:

$$\tau=\mu\frac{du}{dy}$$
  • \(\mu\): dynamic viscosity [Pa·s]
  • \(\nu=\mu/\rho\): kinematic viscosity [m²/s]
Explain that stress is related to deformation rate, not deformation. Use the picture to identify the velocity gradient. Return briefly to toothpaste as a non-Newtonian yield-stress material.

Pressure

$$p=\frac{F_n}{A}$$
  • Scalar field
  • Pressure force acts normal to a surface
  • SI unit: Pa = N/m²
Clarify that the formula is an average definition over a finite area. At a point, pressure is the limiting normal stress in a fluid at rest.

Absolute and Gauge Pressure

$$p_{abs}=p_{atm}+p_g$$

Quick check: If \(p_g=-20\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and \(p_{atm}=101\,\mathrm{kPa}\), find \(p_{abs}\).

Give students 30 seconds. Answer: approximately 81 kPa absolute. Emphasize that negative gauge pressure is not negative absolute pressure.

Compressibility and Vapor Pressure

Bulk modulus

$$K=-V\frac{dp}{dV}$$

Compressibility

$$\beta=-\frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dp}=\frac{1}{K}$$

Vapor pressure

Equilibrium vapor pressure at a given temperature.

If local absolute pressure approaches vapor pressure, cavitation may occur.

Keep this brief. Correct the usual confusion between bulk modulus and compressibility. Link vapor pressure to a future Bernoulli application.

Lecture 1A Takeaways

  • Fluids deform continuously under shear
  • Continuum fields replace molecular tracking
  • Density and specific weight control hydrostatic loading
  • Viscosity relates shear stress to deformation rate
  • Gauge and absolute pressure use different references
Use this as the transition to fluid statics. The next question is how pressure varies when the fluid is at rest.
