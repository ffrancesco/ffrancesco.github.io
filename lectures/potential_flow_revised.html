Fluid Mechanics Course

Lecture 3B · Introduction to Potential Flow

Francesco Ferri

AAU BUILD
Closing 25 to 30 minutes of Lecture 3. This is an introduction, not a complete potential-flow course.

Learning Objectives

  • Define irrotational flow and velocity potential
  • Derive Laplace's equation from incompressibility
  • Define a two-dimensional stream function
  • Use superposition to construct simple ideal flows
  • State the limitations of potential-flow modelling
Read the outcomes briefly. Emphasize that students will use the concepts in calculations, not only recognize definitions.

Why Potential Flow?

  • Provides analytical velocity fields
  • Useful for outer flows where viscous effects are confined to thin regions
  • Supports rapid preliminary analysis
  • Allows superposition of elementary solutions
State clearly that real fluids remain viscous. Potential flow is an ideal-region model, not a claim that viscosity is physically zero everywhere.

Irrotational Velocity Field

Irrotational condition:

$$\nabla\times\mathbf{V}=0$$

In a simply connected region, a scalar velocity potential exists:

$$\mathbf{V}=\nabla\phi$$
Explain curl as local rotation of a fluid element. Note that sign conventions sometimes use V = -grad phi; the course must use one convention consistently.

Incompressibility Leads to Laplace’s Equation

For an incompressible velocity field:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}=0$$

With \(\mathbf{V}=\nabla\phi\):

$$\nabla^2\phi=0$$
This is the core derivation. Explain that Laplace's equation is linear, enabling superposition. Boundary conditions determine the solution.

Two-Dimensional Stream Function

One common convention is:

$$u=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y},\qquad v=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}$$
  • Contours \(\psi=\text{constant}\) are streamlines
  • The definition automatically satisfies 2D incompressible continuity
  • For irrotational flow, \(\psi\) also satisfies Laplace's equation
Mention that an alternative sign convention exists. State the adopted convention on all later slides and exercises.

Elementary Solutions and Superposition

  • Uniform flow
  • Source or sink
  • Vortex
  • Doublet

Because Laplace's equation is linear, sums of solutions remain solutions.

Use only uniform flow plus a doublet as the main example if time is short. Keep source, vortex, and other combinations as reading.

Ideal Flow Around a Cylinder

Uniform flow + doublet produces an ideal cylinder flow.

Once \(\mathbf{V}(x,y)\) is known, Bernoulli gives the pressure field in an irrotational region.

Connect all three lectures: kinematics gives velocity, Bernoulli gives pressure, and integration of pressure gives force.

Limitations

  • Does not resolve viscous boundary layers
  • Cannot predict real separation accurately
  • A non-circulating ideal cylinder has zero drag, d'Alembert's paradox
  • Most useful as an outer-flow or preliminary model
Do not say simply that potential flow is invalid near every wall. It can approximate outer flow while a thin viscous boundary layer enforces no slip.

Three-Lecture Synthesis

Lecture 1: properties + static pressure → hydrostatic structural loads

Lecture 2: velocity + material acceleration → Euler and Bernoulli

Lecture 3: control-volume conservation → flow rates, reactions, and energy transfer

Potential flow: construct ideal velocity fields, then obtain pressure using Bernoulli

Finish by asking students to trace the chain from assumptions to velocity, pressure, and force for one engineering example.
